You've heard of the bdsm test. Probably someone mentioned bdsmtest.org. Maybe you took it yourself — 100 questions, results come back telling you you're 84% submissive, 61% exhibitionist, and 43% rope bunny — and you sat there thinking *okay but what does that mean for us?*

That's the problem. The standard bdsm test is built for one person. It's an inventory. It's useful. It's basically a Myers-Briggs for kink. But when you're in a relationship and you're trying to figure out how the two of you actually fit together — what your dynamic could look like, what you'd both actually enjoy, who naturally gravitates where — a solo personality quiz is about as helpful as both of you getting your astrological charts done and then trying to negotiate a mortgage.

You need a couples test. One you do *together*.

This is that test.

## Why the Solo Test Misses the Point for Couples

Here's the thing about individual kink inventories: they're asking you who you are in a vacuum.

Which is fine, as far as it goes. Self-knowledge is good. But D/s dynamics aren't solo performances. A D/s relationship isn't two people comparing scorecards. It's two people in the same room, at the same time, *reacting to each other*. The chemistry between you matters more than the abstract numbers.

You can score 90% dominant on a test and then sit down across from a specific person and feel a completely different pull. That happens. The human in front of you changes everything. Compatibility isn't calculated from two separate profiles. It's discovered in real interaction — in the moment someone raises an eyebrow and you feel something shift in your chest and you go *oh. oh, interesting.*

That's what this test is designed to surface.

The other thing the solo test can't do: it can't help you and your partner *talk* about this. Most couples don't struggle with figuring out their individual kink profiles. They struggle with the conversation. They struggle with saying it out loud in front of another human being who they're going to see tomorrow at breakfast. This test gives you a structure for that conversation. The scenarios do the work of starting it.

## How This Test Works

Read the scenarios out loud. Both of you.

Don't answer separately and compare scores afterward. That's the wrong approach. The point isn't a spreadsheet. The point is the conversation that happens *around* the scenario — the "wait, I'd actually—" and the pause and the eye contact and whoever goes slightly quieter.

After each scenario, you each say which option fits you. Then you talk about it for ninety seconds. Not to negotiate. Not to convince each other. Just to notice your reactions. Honest answers are the only kind that do anything useful here. Nobody gets to perform the answer they think their partner wants to hear.

One quick note: these scenarios describe preferences and comfort levels, not contracts. Answering "A" to something doesn't mean you've agreed to do it. This is a discovery tool, not a menu. Keep that clear between you — it makes the honest answers a lot easier to give.

Okay. Let's go.

## The 10 Scenarios

### Scenario 1: Who Plans the Night

**The situation:** It's Saturday night. No obligations. You've got the house to yourselves. Nobody has made any plans.

**Option A:** You feel a flicker of anticipation thinking *I could set this whole thing up. Pick the music, the candles, what we're doing, where we start, where it ends. Run it start to finish.* The idea of engineering the night around what you want to happen to them — or what you want them to feel — sounds genuinely appealing. You want to be the one who decides.

**Option B:** You feel a wave of relief thinking *please just tell me what to do tonight.* Not because you're lazy. Because you've been making decisions since 7 AM and the idea of someone else taking the wheel for a few hours — picking the music, setting the mood, deciding when things start — sounds like the best thing that's happened to you this week. You want to be the one who shows up.

### Scenario 2: The Instruction

**The situation:** You're in bed. Things are starting. Your partner leans close and says, in a calm voice: *"I need you to do exactly what I say tonight."*

**Option A:** Your gut reaction is *yes*. Something in your body settles — like you just got permission to stop managing everything — and you say yes before you've even processed it. The idea of following instructions, of being told what to do and doing it, isn't scary. It's the opposite of scary. It's a relief with an edge of heat to it.

**Option B:** Your gut reaction is *I'm going to be the one saying that.* Something in you straightens a little. A calm focus. The idea of being the one who sets the terms, decides the pace, watches your partner follow your lead — that's where your attention naturally goes. You're not anxious. You're *interested*.

### Scenario 3: The Compliment That Wrecks You

**The situation:** Think about the kind of acknowledgment from a partner that would genuinely get to you — the one that would stay with you for days.

**Option A:** It's *"you did so well."* Specifically in that voice. The low one. The calm, deliberate one that carries the weight of someone who doesn't hand out approval casually. You'd want to have earned it. Ideally after something a little difficult — something that took something from you — and then that sentence landing like a hand on your shoulder. *Good.* That word, from the right person, does things to you that should probably be discussed with a professional.

**Option B:** It's the way your partner looks at you after you've taken charge of something. That specific expression — slightly undone, grateful, eyes soft — that says *I was taken care of.* You didn't need the thanks. The look is enough. You're not running it for the praise. You're running it because watching someone feel *held* by a situation you designed is its own thing entirely.

### Scenario 4: The Disagreement

**The situation:** You and your partner are mid-scene and you disagree about something. Not a hard limit situation — just a direction. You wanted to go left; they went right.

**Option A:** You defer. Not out of conflict-avoidance — you've genuinely chosen to give that up for now. You let them have it. There's something clarifying about it: *they're leading, I'm following, that's the deal.* You can file the disagreement away and talk about it afterward. In the moment, you're not in charge of the direction. That's the whole point.

**Option B:** You adjust course but you're the one doing the adjusting. You take in their response, recalibrate, and make a decision. Even if you soften it, even if you check in — you're the one steering. When there's a question of which way to go, you answer it. That's not stubbornness. That's just how you're wired in these spaces.

**The situation:** Someone in this relationship is going to be *seen*. Stripped-down, slightly undone, fully exposed in the emotional sense — no composure, no armor. Who is that?

**Option A:** You. Completely. The idea of falling apart a little — of being seen at full intensity, messy and present and held — sounds exactly right. Not in front of just anyone. But in front of the specific person you're with? Yes. Being the one who gets to let go, who gets to be *completely in it* while someone else holds the container — that's not weakness. That's the whole thing.

**Option B:** Not you. You're the container. You're the person who creates the space where *they* get to fall apart, because you're not. Your composure isn't distance. It's the thing that makes their vulnerability safe. You want to watch them let go. You want to be the reason they could.

### Scenario 6: The Request

**The situation:** Something new comes up — something neither of you has tried. Your partner wants to explore it.

**Option A:** You're interested, but you're waiting to hear how they want to frame it. What role are you in? What are they asking you for? Once you know *your* position — once there's a structure you can inhabit — you can actually evaluate whether you want it. The framework matters. "What do you want me to do?" is a real question, not a deflection.

**Option B:** You're already mentally building the scenario. What would it look like? How would you set it up? What's the pace? You're not imagining being in it — you're imagining *directing* it. Your first move is design, not inquiry. You're thinking about the set before you've cast anyone.

### Scenario 7: The Restraint Scenario

**The situation:** Physical restraint comes up. One person's wrists are held down. The other person is the one holding them.

**Option A:** You're the one with your wrists held. And you're in. Fully. Something about that — the physical fact of not being able to move, of the decision having been made for you — cuts through every other noise in your head. You're not thinking about dinner or the email you forgot to send. You're just *there*. The restraint is a gift. It's the part where you finally get to stop.

You could've done anything. They know that. You know they know that. And they stayed anyway. *That's* the thing. Their choice to stay put is the hottest sentence in this scenario.

**The situation:** Someone establishes a rule for the evening. Something small — *ask before you do that; don't speak unless spoken to; keep your eyes on me.* A rule with a little structure to it.

**Option A:** You feel the shape of the rule and something in you goes *okay*. You're not diminished by it. If anything, you're focused. **Option B:** You're the one who came up with the rule, and the part you're most interested in is watching it land. Do they accept it? Does their body language shift? Did the rule *work?* You're not watching for compliance — you're watching for *response*. Because the response tells you something. It tells you you've made contact.

### Scenario 9: Afterward

**The situation:** The scene ends. Both of you are lying there. The dynamic is fading. You're coming back to yourselves.

**Option A:** You want to be held. Specifically. Warmth, weight, a hand somewhere. Not to be talked to — not yet. Just to be *contained* while your nervous system figures out which way is up. The drop from intensity to ordinary is real and you need a landing pad. Their arms are the landing pad. You don't have to explain it. You just need it.

**Option B:** You want to take care of them. Water. A blanket. Your arm around them. You're watching for what they need and quietly handling it. You came down from the intensity too — you're not a machine — but your landing happens through the act of care. The dynamic is over; the tenderness isn't. You need to make sure they're okay before you can let yourself be okay.

### Scenario 10: The Future

**The situation:** You're imagining a version of your relationship six months from now where things have gotten more intentional — where you've built something. There's more structure, more ritual, more clarity about who does what and when.

**Option A:** What you picture is *knowing your place*. A relationship where you don't have to guess — where the roles are clear, where there's someone you're accountable to who holds you with warmth and firmness. Where *surrender* isn't a word you're embarrassed by. You want a home inside someone else's frame. That sounds, honestly, like peace.

Someone who is yours to take care of, to challenge, to lead — who has chosen you for that role, which is not nothing, which is actually everything. The responsibility of it doesn't scare you. You've been quietly taking responsibility for things your whole life. The difference now is the *consent*. Someone finally *handed you the wheel* out loud.

## Your D/s Profile: 4 Patterns and What They Mean

Count how many A answers and B answers each of you gave.

Mostly A answers = you lean submissive in this dynamic.
Mostly B answers = you lean dominant.
Split (4–6 of each) = you're exploratory — or a switch.

Now look at your two results together.

---

### Pattern 1: One of you leans dominant, one leans submissive

The classic pairing. This is the one most D/s dynamics are built on, and it's called classic for a reason — it works. The chemistry here is in the contrast. One person wants to take the wheel; the other is quietly exhausted from driving.

The next step for you isn't complicated: it's permission. You both already know how you lean. The gap isn't information — it's someone saying it out loud and the other person saying *yes* back. That conversation is the entire first step. If you need a framework for it, the Tension compatibility checklist walks you through it in order.

---

### Pattern 2: Both of you lean submissive

This is more common than people think, and it doesn't mean you're incompatible. It means you're not built for a traditional dynamic — and that's fine. You're built for *scenes*. Scene-based exploration works differently: you take turns. One person steps into the lead for a scene — not because they're naturally dominant, but because they're *willing to hold it for now* — and then you switch. The role is chosen, not identity-based. Some of the best kink play comes out of two people who are both fundamentally submissive negotiating who's going to step up this particular Wednesday night.

The next step for you: get specific about what you each need from a scene, not from a dynamic. The Tension compatibility checklist has a whole section on this.

---

### Pattern 3: Both of you lean dominant

Here's the honest answer: this is the compatibility challenge, and pretending it isn't doesn't help you.

Two dominant people in a D/s dynamic is not impossible. It's just different. You're not building a power structure — you're building a *negotiation practice*. The question for you isn't "who leads?" It's "which of us leads *this?*" Domain-based authority is the frame: you each own certain territories. The erotic tension between two people who are both wired to be in charge can be extraordinary, actually — but only if you stop trying to force a traditional model onto a non-traditional pair.

The next step: start with domain-based division (who owns what in this relationship, in and out of the bedroom) before you try to run a scene. The checklist helps map this.

---

### Pattern 4: One or both of you are split (switches, exploratory, or genuinely unclear)

This is where most couples land, especially early on. The split result isn't a non-answer — it's an honest answer. You're both flexible. You haven't hardened into a fixed role. That's actually an advantage: you can try things, you can switch, you can find out by doing rather than by deciding in advance.

The risk for switch-heavy couples is the ambiguity paralysis. Nobody wants to step up and take the lead because neither person is *sure* they're supposed to. The fix is the checklist — a structured tool for mapping out what you each enjoy, what you're curious about, and where the overlap is. That overlap is your starting point.
