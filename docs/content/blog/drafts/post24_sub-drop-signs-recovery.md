## What Sub Drop Actually Is

Here's the short version: during an intense scene, your brain throws a chemistry party. Dopamine. Oxytocin. Endorphins. Adrenaline. Cortisol. The whole pharmacy. Your nervous system runs at concert volume for an hour, sometimes longer.

Then the scene ends. The lights come up. Your partner falls asleep. The chemistry party doesn't end politely. It crashes. Hard.

What's left is your baseline brain — but your baseline brain is now *lower* than baseline, because your body just spent every neurotransmitter it had on that scene like it was a one-night Vegas trip. Your brain is hungover. Your emotions are running on fumes. Your nervous system is going *"hey, where did all my friends go?"*

This is sub drop.

Same biological event as a runner's drop after a marathon. Same as the blues you get the Monday after a great vacation. Same as the deflation pop stars describe after a sold-out arena tour ends. Same machinery. Different inputs.

It is not weakness. It is not regret. It is not a sign you shouldn't have done the scene. It's just chemistry. Your body wrote a check. Now it's clearing.

The good news: it goes away. The better news: once you know what's happening, it's much, much easier to ride out.

## The Timeline (This Is the Part Nobody Tells You)

Here's what people get wrong. Sub drop is not "I feel weird right after the scene."

Sometimes it shows up immediately, sure. But way more often? It hits **12 to 48 hours later**. Sometimes 72. Some people drop on day three, completely blindsided, having forgotten the scene even happened.

This is why so many people don't realize what's going on. The scene was Friday night. It's now Sunday afternoon. You're crying in the bathtub watching old episodes of *Bridgerton* and you're like "why am I like this." Sub drop. That's why you're like this.

Rough timeline:

- **Hour 0–2 (immediate aftercare window):** You may feel euphoric, floaty, talkative, or shaky. This is normal. Aftercare goes here.
- **Hour 2–12:** Often fine. Sometimes a strange quiet. You go to sleep, you wake up, things seem okay.
- **Hour 12–48 (the danger zone):** This is where most drops happen. Mood plunges. Tearfulness. Self-doubt. Random anxiety. Feeling cold, fragile, or weirdly alone.
- **Hour 48–72:** Tapering. Mood evens out. Energy returns.
- **Day 4+:** Back to baseline. Sometimes with an unusual sense of clarity or closeness to your partner.

If it lasts more than a week, that's not sub drop anymore. That's something else, and you should talk to a therapist. (No shame. It just means you need a different tool.)

## The Signs You're In It

You may not have all of these. You may have all of them. They tend to cluster.

- Sudden, weepy sadness with no clear cause. The kind where a *toilet paper commercial* takes you out.
- Self-doubt: "Were they actually into it?" "Did I do something wrong?" "Do they still respect me?"
- Cold, achy, or fragile body — like a mild flu without the fever.
- Feeling small. Not in the hot way. In the "I want to disappear into a blanket fort" way.
- Anxiety that won't pin to anything specific.
- Unusual hunger or zero appetite. No middle ground.
- Random craving for sweet, salty, or carb-heavy comfort food. (Eat it. Your body is asking.)
- Brain fog. Reading the same sentence four times. Forgetting what you walked into the room for.
- Sudden distance from your partner — even if the scene went perfectly.
- Weirdly intense need for cuddles, voice contact, or reassurance — sometimes from your partner, sometimes from a friend, sometimes from a pet who is now your emotional support animal whether they consented or not.

If you're nodding at three or more of these — yeah. Welcome to drop. You're in good company.

## What Triggers a Bigger Drop

Three main factors. The more boxes you check, the harder the landing.

**1. Intensity.** Bigger scenes, deeper headspace, more adrenaline = bigger crash. The more you went up, the more you come down. This is just gravity wearing a different outfit.

**2. Vulnerability.** Scenes that touched something emotional, not just physical. Crying scenes, humiliation play, anything that opened a door you usually keep shut. Your nervous system was very brave. Now it's going to lie on the couch about it for a day.

**3. Time without partner contact.** This is the sneaky one. Drops get *worse* when there's been no check-in. No text. No "hey, are you good?" No reassurance that the scene didn't change how they see you. Your brain interprets silence as confirmation of every dark thing it's currently inventing.

Bonus triggers:
- Skipped aftercare ("I'm fine, you can sleep") — you weren't fine, you were endorphin-drunk.
- Returning to a stressful week (work, family drama) without a buffer day.
- Alcohol after the scene. (It feels like a reward. It is not a reward. It is a tax.)
- Dehydration. Your body sweat out more than you think.
- Skipped meals. You can't run on fumes when the fumes are already gone.

## The Actual Recovery Protocol

This is the part you came here for. Bookmark this. Send it to your partner. Stick it on the fridge under a magnet you've never explained to your mother.

### First 24 Hours After the Scene

- **Water.** Two liters minimum. More than you think. Your body is dehydrated from sweat, adrenaline, and emotional output.
- **Real food.** Protein and carbs. Eggs, pasta, chicken, rice. Not a salad. Not a smoothie. *Food*. Your nervous system needs fuel that takes effort to digest.
- **Sleep.** Whatever you normally sleep, add one or two hours. If you can't add hours, at least take a nap. Your brain is rebuilding.
- **Light contact with your partner.** A text, a voice note, a hand on the back. Doesn't need to be intense. Just needs to be *there*.
- **No big decisions.** Don't text your ex. Don't quit your job. Don't have the "where is this relationship going" conversation. Your judgment is currently being held together by Scotch tape and dopamine residue.

### 24 to 48 Hours

This is when most drops hit hardest. Plan ahead so you're not making decisions from inside the drop.

- **Move gently.** A walk. A stretch. A bath. Nothing intense. Your nervous system already had its workout.
- **Comfort input.** Comfort show. Comfort book. Comfort food. Pedro Pascal in *anything*. The point is sensory input that does not require you to be smart or interesting.
- **Voice contact with your partner.** Not text. *Voice*. If they're not available, voice with a trusted friend (about anything — they don't need the scene details). Hearing a human voice that loves you reroutes the nervous system in a way text just can't.
- **Light sun if you can get it.** Even ten minutes outside in daylight. Sunlight resets the same chemicals you just spent.
- **Permission to be useless.** You're not broken. You're not failing at adulthood. You're recovering. Athletes get rest days. You get a rest day.

### 48 to 72 Hours

Things should be lifting. If they're not, here's the move:

- **Talk to your partner about the scene.** A real conversation. What worked, what didn't, how you both felt. Not a debrief in the technical sense — just a *conversation*. This integrates the experience and tells your brain "we are okay, the scene didn't break us."
- **Get back to a normal rhythm.** Normal sleep window. Normal meals. Normal workout if that's your thing. Your body wants the rails back under it.
- **Note what worked.** Brief journal entry. "Drop hit at hour 30. Sleep + soup + partner voice note pulled me out by hour 50." Next time you'll know.

## How Your Partner Should Help

Forward this section to them. No, really. Right now.

If your partner just topped you in a scene, they have a job for the next 72 hours, and the job is not complicated:

1. **Check in within 12 hours, then again at 24, then at 48.** A simple text. "Hey, how are you feeling?" Not "Are you in drop?" — that's a clinical question. Just human contact.
2. **Believe the answer.** If they say "weird," that's data. Don't argue them out of it. Don't reassure them in a way that shuts the conversation down. Just listen.
3. **Offer specifics, not options.** "I'm bringing soup at 7" beats "let me know if you need anything." A subby in drop cannot organize a request. They can only accept or decline an offer.
4. **Voice or in-person contact at least once during the 48-hour window.** Not just text. Voice resets the nervous system. Your texts cannot.
5. **Don't take it personally.** If they pull away, withdraw, or get weird — it's not you. It's the chemistry. Hold space. Don't chase. Don't panic.
6. **No new scenes during the drop window.** Even if they ask. *Especially* if they ask. Their judgment is impaired. Be the grown-up.

If you're the partner and you're reading this thinking "wow, I had no idea, I'm a bad partner" — you're not a bad partner. You just didn't know. Now you know. Welcome to actually doing this well.

## FAQ

**Is sub drop a sign I shouldn't be doing BDSM?**

No. It's a sign you played hard enough to need recovery. Marathoners drop. Touring musicians drop. People who do hard, beautiful things have drops. The fix isn't to stop — it's to plan for it.

**Why didn't I drop after my first few scenes?**

You may have been running on adrenaline and novelty. You may not have gone deep enough yet. Some people don't drop until they hit a certain depth of trust with a partner — your body wouldn't let go before it felt safe. That's actually a green flag.

**Can dominants get drop too?**

Yes. It's called dom drop, and it's just as real. Sometimes worse, because dominants are less likely to ask for care. We'll cover that in a separate article. (Short version: if you topped, you also need water, food, sleep, and a friendly voice. Don't be a hero.)

**Is sub drop the same as regret?**

No. Regret is "I wish that hadn't happened." Drop is "that happened and now my chemistry is upside down for two days." Regret comes from the head. Drop comes from the brainstem. Different floors of the building.

**How do I tell my partner I'm dropping without making them panic?**

Try: "I'm dropping a little. Nothing went wrong — my body is just catching up. Can you stay close today?" That sentence is gold. Save it.

**What if I drop and I'm alone?**

Build a drop kit before you need one. Water, electrolytes, a soft snack, a playlist that calms you, a list of two friends you can text (not about the scene, just to be in voice contact). Future-you will thank past-you.

**Will it get easier over time?**

Yes. Once you know what's happening, drops feel less like an emergency and more like weather. Bad weather, sure. But you stop confusing the storm for the end of the world.
